## What Is Chicago Citation Style?

Chicago style is a system of documentation that explains how to acknowledge books, journal articles, websites, book chapters, and other sources in academic writing.

It offers two principal systems:

- **Notes & Bibliography:** Uses numbered footnotes or endnotes, usually accompanied by a bibliography.
- **Author-Date:** Uses parenthetical citations in the text and a corresponding reference list.

The appropriate system depends on the requirements of your instructor, institution, journal, or publisher.

## Notes & Bibliography vs. Author-Date

Although both systems follow Chicago style, they present source information differently.

### Notes & Bibliography

Notes & Bibliography is commonly associated with the humanities, particularly history, literature, and the arts. It uses numbered notes to identify sources, with a bibliography listing the works consulted or cited.

For example, a full note for a book might look like this:

Charles Yu, *Interior Chinatown* (Pantheon Books, 2020), 45.

A shortened note for a later citation could look like this:

Yu, *Interior Chinatown*, 48.

The bibliography entry would be:

Yu, Charles. *Interior Chinatown*. Pantheon Books, 2020.

These examples demonstrate how the same source can appear in different formats depending on where and how it is cited.

For more detailed instructions, see the guide to [Chicago Notes & Bibliography](https://chicagoreferencegenerator.com/), which explains full notes, shortened notes, and bibliography entries.

### Author-Date

The Author-Date system is frequently used in the sciences and social sciences. It places a brief citation in parentheses within the text and provides the complete source details in a reference list.

For the same book, the in-text citation could be:

(Yu 2020, 45)

The corresponding reference-list entry would be:

Yu, Charles. 2020. *Interior Chinatown*. Pantheon Books.

The author and publication year help readers identify the source in the reference list.

## How to Cite Different Types of Sources in Chicago Style

The correct citation format depends on the type of source you are using. Books, journal articles, websites, and chapters have different bibliographic details.

### Citing a Book

Book citations generally include the author, title, publisher, and publication year. Depending on the source and citation system, additional information may be required.

For a book, verify the author, complete title, edition when relevant, publisher, publication year, and any relevant URL or ISBN information.

You can use the [Chicago book citation guide](https://chicagoreferencegenerator.com/) to review the information required for book references.

### Citing a Journal Article

Journal articles commonly require the author's name, article title, journal title, volume, issue, publication date or year, page information where applicable, and DOI when available.

For example, a DOI can make it easier to identify the correct scholarly article and retrieve its metadata.

### Citing a Website

Website citations can be more complicated because webpages do not always display all the information needed for a reference.

Depending on the source, relevant details may include the individual author or organization, page title, website name, publication or revision date, access information, and URL.

### Citing a Chapter in an Edited Book

A chapter citation must distinguish the chapter's author from the author or editor responsible for the larger book.

You should check the chapter title, book title, editor information, publisher, publication year, and any other details required by the applicable Chicago format.

## What Changed in Chicago 18th Edition?

The 18th edition of *The Chicago Manual of Style* was published in 2024 and introduced changes affecting several citation practices.

Some important areas to review include:

**Publication place in book citations:** A place of publication is no longer normally required for modern book citations.

**Chapters in edited books:** Standard bibliography and reference-list entries no longer require the chapter's page range, although page numbers or other locators can still be important when citing a particular passage.

**Author lists:** The rules for shortening long lists of authors have changed.

**Shortened citations:** The edition updates guidance on how shortened citations should be handled.

**Journal citations:** Some journal-date conventions and related citation details require attention to the current rules.

These changes matter because older citation examples and tools may follow conventions from the 17th edition. Always check that your references follow the edition required by your institution.

For a more focused explanation, consult the [Chicago 18th Edition guide](https://chicagoreferencegenerator.com/).

## How to Format Chicago Footnotes and Bibliographies

Footnotes, endnotes, and bibliographies serve different purposes in Notes & Bibliography.

A full note generally provides the information required to identify a source when it is cited for the first time. A shortened note identifies the same source more concisely in subsequent citations. A bibliography presents the sources in an organized list.

For example, the first note might include an author's full name and the book's complete title. A later note can use the author's surname and a shortened title.

When preparing a bibliography, pay attention to alphabetical order, punctuation, italics, and the formatting of entries. Consistency is important, particularly in longer research papers.

You can learn more about these requirements in the [Chicago Footnotes guide](https://chicagoreferencegenerator.com/) and the [Chicago Bibliography guide](https://chicagoreferencegenerator.com/).
